*** TLDR
A program-integrity analyst shares six SQL patterns for fraud detection: velocity checks (too many transactions too fast), impossible travel (Chicago to LA in 7 minutes), suspicious amounts ($1.00 card tests, $99.99 to avoid ID checks), merchant anomaly spikes, off-hours transactions outside cardholder habits, and composable window functions. The author argues that fraud detection is mostly SQL, not ML or graph databases, and the key is making analysts able to express hypotheses as filters instead of engineering tickets.

*** Take
Finally, someone admits that most fraud detection is just GROUP BY and window functions, not a $200k/year ML engineer building a neural network that does the same thing with extra steps. The impossible travel query alone would catch half the fraud that fancy systems miss because nobody bothered to check if someone teleported.

*** TLDR
John Carlos Baez explains that quasicrystals (crystal-like structures with patterns that never repeat, like Penrose tiles) are extraordinarily rare in nature. Only four locations have produced them: a single meteorite in far-east Russia (Khatyrka) that contained three different types including icosahedrite, a lightning strike on a sand dune near a downed power line in Nebraska, and the Trinity atomic bomb test site. The rest are lab-made. These form only under extreme conditions: ultra-high-velocity asteroid collisions, lightning-induced rapid quenching, or nuclear explosions.

*** Take
The universe really doesn't want quasicrystals to exist. You need either a meteor collision, a bolt of lightning hitting a downed power line in Nebraska (not just any lightning, mind you), or literally an atomic bomb. Nature had 4.5 billion years and managed to make exactly three compositions naturally. Meanwhile, labs crank them out like Tuesday.
