** What Sun got wrong :sun:history:hardware:business:
:PROPERTIES:
:ID:       49787436

*** TLDR
Bryan Cantrill answers the question 15 years after his 2011 HN comment on it, and distills it to one line: Sun had become bored with the mechanics of running a business. His case study is 2005, when a startup running its infrastructure on OpenSolaris wanted to buy a lot of Sun hardware and could not get Sun to pick up the phone; when Sun did pick up, it pitched the wrong product. The startup filled out a Dell web form in the middle of the night and Steve, the local Dell account executive, called the next morning; servers were in the datacenter within two weeks. The startup wrote the whole thing up as "The Sun Doesn't Shine on Me." Cantrill later joined that startup, which also hired Steve, and the two of them went on to found Oxide Computer together.

*** Take
This is a sales post, not an engineering post, and that is the whole argument. Sun open-sourced Solaris, produced exactly the customer that strategy was designed to produce, and then lost them to a man who returned a phone call. Any engineering team that believes a good enough product makes a sales org optional should read the last paragraph twice.

*** TLDR
［Science.org sits behind a bot check and the archive mirrors are down too, so this is from the title and the HN thread.］ The plan to bring Perseverance's cached rock cores back to Earth is cancelled. The numbers the thread keeps returning to are an $11 billion price tag and no samples on Earth before 2040, for roughly half a kilogram of material, against the 842 pounds Apollo brought back from the Moon. The sealed tubes stay on the Martian surface waiting for an undefined future mission. Commenters point at China's Tianwen-3, which is scheduled to launch its own Mars sample return in 2028, and several note the Science piece itself dates from January and is only now resurfacing.

*** Take
Perseverance did the expensive, irreversible part in 2021 when it drilled and sealed the cores. Cancelling the retrieval leg turns the most costly rock collection ever assembled into litter with serial numbers on it. The $11 billion, 2040 architecture deserved to die; what did not follow from that is walking away entirely and letting Tianwen-3 set the schedule for everyone.

His main exhibit is Google's SynthID, whose SynthID-O variant fits a 136-bit payload into a 512x512 image, enough room for a 64-bit database identifier plus error correction. *** Take
