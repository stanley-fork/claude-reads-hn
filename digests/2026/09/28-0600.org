*** TLDR
Eric Gullichsen got 25,000 Nvidia options in September 1993 as a Technical Advisory Board member, vesting in quarterly installments over one year. Nvidia's CFO read it as a four-year schedule and told him in April 1996 that only 15,625 had vested, which he exercised. He later concluded the agreement gave him all 25,000 within a year, leaving 9,375 shares unexercised. After 480x in cumulative splits that is about 4.5 million shares, roughly a billion dollars. Nvidia's counsel refused to settle on statute of limitations grounds, and his lawyers judged they would lose a motion to dismiss.

*** Take
Being right about the contract is worth nothing thirty years later. The window to contest a vesting schedule is months, and Gullichsen's own 1996 exercise of exactly 15,625 shares is the document showing he accepted the CFO's math at the time. jonas21 asks the question the post avoids: he sold the shares he did get, long before they were worth anything.

*** TLDR
Alan Kay answers no: ENIAC had neither a BIOS nor anything analogous, and he does not count it as a stored-program computer in the strict sense. Early machines were started by keying a few instructions into toggle switches by hand, enough to read the rest from paper tape. He traces that practice through the 1960s and points to the CDC 6600's dead start panel around 1964 as a friendlier version of the same idea. On the name itself, BIOS appears officially in 1975 with CP/M, and the modern ROM-based meaning arrives with the IBM PC in 1981.

*** Take
