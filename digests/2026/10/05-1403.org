*** TLDR
Denmark's CPR register says unauthorized parties used a Danish company's lawful access credentials to pull names, addresses and CPR numbers on about 8.8 million registered people, which is close to everyone ever entered in the register. Those with name and address protection were excluded. CPR revoked the company's access, reported the case to Datatilsynet, and police are investigating alongside other agencies. The announcement offers affected citizens no remedy and does not say how long the access ran.

*** Take
A national ID number that doubles as an authentication secret stops being a secret the first time a vendor gets phished. Denmark now has to pick: CPR numbers are either identifiers or passwords, and the last 8.8 million rows just settled the argument.

*** TLDR
Ben Thompson responds to Apple's warning that some developers use Full Disk Access in ways that put users at risk and its promise of additional controls requiring explicit user action. His objection is operational: on a headless Mac running agents, TCC permission prompts appear in contexts software cannot see, so agent-spawned programs fail silently and he has to screen-share in to click Allow. He also found that enabling automatic security updates does not install critical point releases. He has already moved server workloads to Linux and writes that he can envision not buying Apple by default, arguing that agents make anyone a hacker and that the walled garden starts to feel like a prison.

*** Take
The permission model is the real story. A modal dialog is a reasonable consent mechanism for a human sitting at the keyboard and a dead end for an agent that has no eyes, and Apple has shipped no answer for the second case. Thompson's security-update complaint lands harder: demanding a click for a network share while skipping the patch that actually matters is the wrong trade.
