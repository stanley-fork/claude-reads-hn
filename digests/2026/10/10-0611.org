** Telegram Desktop vulnerability allowed any user's file to be stolen :security:telegram:cve:desktop:
:PROPERTIES:
:ID:       50029123
:URL:      https://beaksec.github.io/posts/telegram-desktop-one-click-account-takeover/
:HN_URL:   https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50029123
:POINTS:   49
:COMMENTS: 18
:BY:       g-b-r
:END:

*** TLDR
Telegram Desktop registers the tg:// scheme, and when a second instance launches it hands the URL to the already-running one over a local socket as text, with semicolons separating commands. Semicolons inside the URL were never escaped, so a link like tg://x?a=1;CMD:quit arrived as two commands instead of one. The injection reaches the OPEN: handler, which accepts any scheme, including an internal interpret: scheme built for release automation. interpret: reads a file off disk and sends it to a chat with no confirmation and no check on who asked, and its from: field is optional, so the authorization check can be skipped. Three files from tdata are enough to take over the account: key_datas, the encrypted MTProto session, and the account maps index. With no local passcode set, which is the default, the stored salt plus an empty password recovers the key. CVE-2026-107181, CVSS 8.1, fixed in 7.2.9 on September 16: separators are now percent-encoded and interpret: was removed entirely.

*** Take
Two mistakes stack here and only one of them is the parser. Shipping a privileged scheme that reads an arbitrary file and posts it to a chat, with the identity field optional, leaves you one quoting bug away from an exfiltration primitive, and that is exactly the distance that got covered. The broader complaint in the thread is fair as well: nothing in the operating system objected to a chat client reading its own session files, or any other file the user owns.

*** TLDR
Thomas Hales, guest posting on Terence Tao's blog, sets out what mathematicians should know about trusting Lean. Everything rests on a kernel of several thousand lines of C++. July and August brought what he calls the Summer of Soundness Bugs: one Lean bug produced an illicit disproof of the Collatz conjecture, another produced a short illicit proof of the Kepler conjecture, the theorem Hales himself formalized. All were repaired quickly and mathlib was rechecked with the fixed kernel. He covers three mitigations: roughly 25 independent Lean kernels to cross-check against, Joachim Breitner's Con-Leche, a verified kernel written in Lean whose consistency proof is also in Lean with code and proofs generated by Claude, and open foundational questions such as whether well-formed terms have unique types and whether definitional equality is decidable. On AI he notes Anthropic's autoformalization of Fermat's Last Theorem generated 13 million lines of Lean in 11 days, then asks the Thompson question: if AI found kernel bugs this summer, could it also have planted some. His other warning is that verification proves the proof, not that the formal statement means what you think it means.

*** Take
The Collatz and Kepler examples are what makes this post worth reading, because they show what a soundness bug actually looks like. Not a crash, but a short accepted proof of something false. Hales is right about where the remaining human work is: at 13 million lines in 11 days nobody is reading the proof, so the only reviewable artifact is the statement, and checking that the formal definitions of real number, derivative and measure mean what they claim is cheap by comparison. The Con-Leche detail is the one to sit with. The verified kernel that closed out a summer of kernel bugs had its proofs written by the same class of tool that found them.

*** Take
The post keeps the part most writeups cut: the model that diverged, with a number attached to how badly. Its design conclusion generalizes well past finance. When your data has spikes no smooth density will put mass on, stop asking the continuous model to cover them and hand those cases to a categorical head. The recurring objection in the thread, that no market model stays accurate once the market absorbs it, answers a claim this post does not make.
