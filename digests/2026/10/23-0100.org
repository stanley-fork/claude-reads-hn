*** TLDR
Amanda Smith, a Pennsylvania woman, faces up to six years on charges of computer fraud, unlawful use of a communication device and theft, over a Cash App account a scammer opened with her Social Security number and date of birth. The scammer used a hacked Facebook Marketplace seller account to take $1,000 from a buyer in Arkansas, then split the money across accounts within hours. Police got Smith's name from Cash App under a search warrant and charged her; she spent three nights in jail. Cash App refused to show her the account because she could not prove she owned it, and she only saw the records through criminal discovery. Those records list an address she does not recognize, an iPhone running an older iOS than her own phone, and a phone number tied to past breaches and to a Venmo account under a different name. Prosecutors offered a $1,000 fine, $1,000 restitution and three years' probation. She refused. Her lawyer is seeking dismissal ahead of a November hearing.

*** Take
The only thing linking her to the account is the name typed into a signup form, and the device records that contradict it were in the file before charges were filed. The plea offer is the tell: three years' probation closes the case quietly, while a dismissal would put in writing that the arrest was wrong.

*** TLDR
NVIDIA released OpenShell under Apache 2.0: a runtime that gives each autonomous agent its own kernel-isolated sandbox with policies over filesystem, syscall and network access, and credential providers that only hand over secrets at approved endpoints. The second layer is formal verification of policy changes, which flags risky new permissions such as reaching a previously unapproved host while holding credentials, and routes them to a human. *** Take
The sandbox is the part everyone already has, usually a container and a deny list. The part worth looking at is the prover that blocks a policy diff granting credentialed access to a new host, because that is the exact change a careless agent pushes and nobody reads.
