*** TLDR
A federal judge granted a preliminary injunction against the VPN provisions of Utah's SB 73, which required adult sites to either block VPN traffic or establish a visitor's real physical location, and also barred sites from explaining how to use a VPN. Judge Barlow wrote that the amended statute "essentially imposes strict liability" and that compliance would require "geolocation perfection," which "is not presently possible." EFF argued the detection methods on offer, such as reading latency or device time zones, are unreliable and would push invasive data collection onto users everywhere, including outside Utah. Utah legislators have signaled they may rewrite the law next session.

*** Take
The ruling matters less for the First Amendment line than for the engineering one: the court treated "this cannot be built" as a reason a statute fails, not as a compliance detail for defendants to solve. Expect the rewrite to swap the verbs for "commercially reasonable efforts" and keep the same impossible requirement, because that phrasing survives this opinion.

***** Title

***** TLDR

El juez Barlow escribió que la norma reformada "impone en esencia responsabilidad objetiva" y que cumplirla exigiría una "perfección en la geolocalización" que "no es posible hoy". La EFF argumentó que los métodos de detección disponibles, como medir la latencia o leer la zona horaria del dispositivo, no son fiables y forzarían una recolección invasiva de datos sobre usuarios de todo el mundo, no solo de Utah. La reescritura cambiará los verbos por "esfuerzos comercialmente razonables" y mantendrá el mismo requisito imposible, porque esa formulación sobrevive a esta sentencia.

*** TLDR
At Kernel Recipes 2026, Greg Kroah-Hartman took apart the headline that Anthropic's Mythos found 79 Linux vulnerabilities: 24 reports had no detail, 14 were not bugs, 3 contained invented data, 15 were already fixed in the latest release (4 of those by Anthropic), and of the remainder 20 needed fixes, 7 of which assumed a malicious filesystem image, which the kernel's documented threat model excludes. His count of real bug fixes was 10, which he put at about an hour of kernel development. Kernel CVEs now run roughly 33 a day, up from 50 a week a year ago, and he said mean time to exploit has gone from 63 days in 2018 to negative today. His advice: push back immediately on reports padded with long changelogs and comments, assume anything you send a hosted model will surface in someone else's report, point reporters at the documented threat model, and use local models anyway. He cited Coverity's finding that a false positive rate above 20% gets a tool ignored. ［Video is behind a CAPTCHA for us; details from the Kernel Recipes live blog of the talk and the slides quoted in the thread.］

*** Take
The 79-to-10 gap is a reporting failure, not a capability result: pattern-matching decades of kernel fixes against unpatched code is a legitimate technique, and 10 fixes in a week of compute is fine work. Shipping it as 79 vulnerabilities is what burns the maintainers who have to triage it, and Greg's Coverity citation is the real warning shot: tools do not get rejected for being wrong, they get rejected for being wrong 20% of the time while sounding certain.
