*** TLDR
OpenAI announced three results produced with its models. In mathematics, a proof of the local smoothing conjecture in all dimensions, which also settles the Kakeya conjecture and Stein's restriction conjecture, using a new auxiliary PDE. In computer science, a disproof of the Exponential Time Hypothesis, an assumption hundreds of complexity papers are built on. In physics, a rigorous existence proof for quantum chromodynamics including pure Yang-Mills theory, one of the Clay Millennium problems. Named academics were brought in before publication: Russell Impagliazzo and Ramamohan Paturi (UC San Diego) and Ryan Williams (MIT) on complexity; David Gross with Michael Douglas, Arthur Jaffe, Igor Klebanov and Nikita Nekrasov on physics. The math result was released with no formal collaboration. Proofs were formalized in Lean, dated September to October 2026. The post does not say which models produced them.

*** Take
Bringing the mathematicians in before the press release is the actual change from OpenAI's last math announcement, and it is the part worth copying. The ETH disproof is the substantive item, and the paper is more careful than the blog post: the bound does not transfer under general polynomial-time reductions, so NP problems do not become subexponential. That caveat is louder in the HN thread than in the announcement. One commenter points at an Anthropic proof with the same bounds by a different route, which would make the result about the problem being tractable rather than about one lab's model.

*** TLDR
Jan van den Berg revisits Brooks's Law for agent-based development. He grants the obvious objection first: agents have no onboarding delay and none of the social coordination overhead that made adding people expensive. Then he argues the core constraint does not move, because Brooks's point about sequential work still holds. Nine months is nine months regardless of how many agents you assign. What changes is where the overhead lands: a human still has to define what done means, review output, catch hallucinations and integrate results, so the management cost shifts from people to agents. His new failure mode is agents on unclear requirements rewriting each other's work in a fast loop. His 2026 restatement of the law: "Adding agents to a project with unclear requirements gets you the wrong answer faster."

*** Take
The restatement is sharper than the post arguing for it, which mostly re-narrates Brooks. The better idea is in the thread: rimeice flips the conclusion by pointing out that a wrong answer arriving in two minutes is now a cheap way to discover what the requirements actually are, which is a real workflow and not what the author intended. Nine agents overwriting each other is a coordination failure a nine-person team would also have, so it argues for the same fix Brooks did rather than for a new law.

*** Comments

**** rimeice
"Adding agents to a project with unclear requirements gets you the wrong answer faster." The thing is it gets you the wrong answer so fast it's now the best way to figure out the right requirements.

**** jleask
Coordination was the key issue Brooks identified: the more people working in a finite area, the more they need to coordinate and communicate. Throw nine autonomous agents at a single codebase without crystal-clear orchestration and they will happily rewrite each other's work in a high-speed loop. Humans often do this too.
