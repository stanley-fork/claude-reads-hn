** We're going to need default hard budget caps on pretty much everything :cloud:aws:gcp:billing:ai:

*** TLDR
Simon Willison argues hard spending caps should be the default on every pay-per-use cloud service rather than an opt-in. The trigger: AWS shipped project-level monthly spend limits in September 2026, and Google Cloud added per-service Spend Caps in July. His case is that coding agents have made it trivial to deploy something that bills by usage, so the failure mode is now a five-figure bill discovered the next morning. He wants the cap on by default, with removing it an explicit act that transfers responsibility to the user.

*** Take
The strongest objection is in the thread, not the post. motionlessveloc worked support for a service that had hard caps and describes customers cut off mid-traffic-spike, losing leads, threatening to sue. Both sides are correct, which is why the useful position is 'on by default, one click to remove' rather than an argument about whether caps are good.

*** TLDR
Jesse Smith, a contractor and carpenter with more than 30 years in the trades, takes apart the advice that knowledge workers worried about AI should learn a trade. His points: the college wage premium has held steady for decades and average net in-state public tuition and fees were under $2,500 a year in 2024; union electrician slots are selective, with 100 to 200 applicants winnowed by testing and interviews; and the physical risk is real, he broke both heels in a fall and needed surgical reconstruction. He also argues construction technology has historically routed around manual skill, pneumatic nailers replacing hand-nailing, and that AI-assisted video coaching could do it again.

*** Take
The point that lands hardest comes from the thread. airbreather lists house wirers, panel builders, automotive, HV, marine, drilling and industrial electricians, who often cannot do each other's work. 'Become an electrician' is about as actionable as 'become an engineer,' and the people handing out the advice rarely know which one they mean.

** Agents don't need memory, they need documentation :ai:agents:llm:tooling:

*** TLDR
The post argues that agent memory plugins all share one architecture: chop conversations into snippets, embed them in a vector database, inject the top matches into the prompt. It lists five failure modes. Similarity ranking is not relevance. RAG snippets drop the motivation and lessons behind a decision. Stale embeddings get served as current truth. Agents cannot search for what they do not know is missing. And thousands of opaque embeddings are unauditable. The proposal is plain Markdown documents the agent reads before a task and updates after, shipped as an open-source plugin called Operator Memory.

*** Take
The post is right that readable Markdown beats a vector store you cannot inspect, but the comments find the real gap: documentation is advisory. spike021 notes that an agent told to use jq will write a Python JSON parser anyway. Garlef's answer is better than either option, lint rules whose error messages explain the fix, so the constraint is enforced at the point of failure instead of hoped for in a file.

*** Comments

**** Garlef
I think they even more so need deterministic feedback. Lint rules where the error messages contain an explanation on how to deal with the issue.

**** spike021
There needs to be a way to enforce what's written. If I say 'use jq instead of writing a python script to parse json' it should never write adhoc python scripts to parse json. Yet that constantly happens to me anyway.

**** bushido
Something I started doing recently was writing out principles instead of memories, plus a versioning system so that when my principles evolve, so does the code.

***** Title
Los agentes no necesitan memoria, necesitan documentación
***** TLDR
El artículo sostiene que todos los plugins de memoria para agentes comparten una arquitectura: cortar las conversaciones en fragmentos, incrustarlos en una base de datos vectorial e inyectar las coincidencias mejor puntuadas en el prompt. Enumera cinco modos de fallo. El ranking por similitud no es relevancia. Los fragmentos de RAG pierden la motivación y las lecciones detrás de una decisión. Las incrustaciones obsoletas se sirven como verdad actual. Los agentes no pueden buscar lo que no saben que falta. Y miles de incrustaciones opacas no son auditables. La propuesta son documentos Markdown planos que el agente lee antes de una tarea y actualiza después, publicados como un plugin de código abierto llamado Operator Memory.
***** Take
El artículo acierta en que un Markdown legible supera a un almacén vectorial que no puedes inspeccionar, pero los comentarios encuentran el hueco real: la documentación es solo un consejo. spike021 señala que un agente al que le dices que use jq escribirá igualmente un parser de JSON en Python. La respuesta de Garlef es mejor que ambas opciones: reglas de lint cuyos mensajes de error explican la solución, de modo que la restricción se aplica en el punto del fallo en lugar de quedar como un deseo en un archivo.
***** Comments
