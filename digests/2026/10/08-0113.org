*** TLDR
OpenAI released GPT-6, with GPT-6 Sol on paid tiers and GPT-6 Luna for free users, plus Intelligent UI: ChatGPT answers can now mix text with generated interactive components such as charts, forms, buttons and diagrams, with the model choosing the format. The reported numbers are about latency and search rather than raw capability: GPT-6 Instant starts answering web search queries 44% sooner on average than GPT-5.6 Instant, and GPT-6 Extra High scores higher than GPT-5.6 Extra High while responding at GPT-5.6 Medium's speed. OpenAI also claims better resistance to attempts to bypass its safety training and better recognition of its own limits. Rollout is immediate for Plus, Pro, Business and Enterprise, with Free and Go the next day. No new pricing tiers.

*** Take
Intelligent UI is the announcement; GPT-6 is the version number attached to it. The demos are the argument and also the weak spot, because several of them beat strawmen that do not exist: real paint chips have the color printed on them, real city guides come with maps. The thread's main objection is taste rather than capability. Illustrated, checklist-shaped answers with a lot of whitespace read as condescending to someone who asked a technical question, and the launch ships no way to turn it off.

*** TLDR
An illustrated history of how the industrial revolution bootstrapped accuracy, written as a sequel to the author's beam engine piece and prompted by Watt's trouble getting a true cylinder bore. In the 1760s cylinders were off by around 3/8 inch across an 18-inch diameter. John Wilkinson's 1775 boring mill supported the cutting bar at both ends so it could not sag, and in 1776 produced a 50-inch cylinder erring by no more than the thickness of an old shilling, roughly one part in a thousand. Henry Maudslay's screw-cutting lathe drove the tool from a lead screw through change wheels, making threads reproducible without hand guidance, and his bench micrometer, nicknamed the Lord Chancellor, read thousandths of an inch. The three-plate method produced flat surfaces with no flat reference to copy. By the 1850s Joseph Whitworth's measuring machine resolved a millionth of an inch, about 25 nanometres, and by 1857 his shop floor worked to twenty-thousandths. The argument is a feedback loop: each generation of machines cut the parts for better machines, multiplying accuracy roughly a thousandfold in fifty years.

*** Take
The best thing in here is Maudslay's averaging trick: gear two imperfect lead screws to turn together, join their nuts with a bar, fix the cutting tool at the midpoint, and the tool follows the average of the two, so where one runs ahead and the other lags the errors partly cancel. That is error correction built in metal, long before anyone wrote it down as mathematics, and it is the mechanism behind the whole bootstrap. One fair complaint from the thread: the piece uses precision and accuracy loosely, and for a lay audience it should define them up front.
