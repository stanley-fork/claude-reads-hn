*** TLDR
You say numbers from 1 to 9 out loud, as randomly as you can. Before each one the page seals a prediction in a virtual envelope; the first ten are a warm-up that does not count. Under the hood it runs ten small predictors at once and trusts whichever has been right lately: which number follows which, runs going up or down, repeats and clusters, and which numbers you have been neglecting. The author says the neglect predictor does most of the work. At the end it scores you against 206 people from a psychology study.

*** Take
The neglect predictor is the whole finding. People treat randomness as a quota to fill, so they top up whichever digit has not come around in a while, and that single habit is enough to beat them. It also explains the split in the thread: commenters who typed their numbers scored 11 to 12%, barely above the 11% you would get by chance, because a keyboard lets you stall and check yourself in a way that speaking out loud does not.

*** TLDR
A budgeting app for Apple Silicon Macs, in beta, that answers questions about your spending with models running on-device. You import a CSV from any bank; a small local model turns your question into a structured query of what to count, about what and over which dates, and hand-written code does the arithmetic against your ledger. A second local reader, a 2.5 GB download, interprets the question independently, and the app only shows an answer when both agree, otherwise it says it is not sure. The write-up reports wrong answers going from roughly 1 in 5 to 5 out of 494, and lists the costs: 12 to 14 seconds per answer instead of 0.1, and tests run on nine invented households rather than real finances.

*** Take
The most useful number in the write-up is one of the failures: a small model fine-tuned on questions the developers wrote themselves hit 97% in-house and dropped to 25% on households written by people who had never seen the code. Anyone shipping a tuned small model should read that twice. Three-model majority voting also failed, at 20 to 22% error, which is why the design ended at two readers plus a refusal. The product risk is elsewhere: as the top commenter says, CSV export flows differ at every bank, and that is where most users will quit before any of this accuracy matters.

*** Comments
